01
The Global Tapestryc. 1200–1450 · regional states, belief systems, legitimacy, social organization.
8–10%ComparisonContext

Big question

How did states build and maintain power while religion, social structure, and regional traditions shaped different parts of the world?

Anchor connections

Song China: bureaucracy and Confucian traditions. South/Southeast Asia: diverse Hindu, Buddhist, and Islamic states. Americas: tribute/labor systems such as those used by the Mexica and Inca.

Do not flatten the unit: The point is not “every civilization had a government.” Compare the specific tools of rule, belief, social hierarchy, and economic organization.
Retrieve: Explain one way Song China strengthened state administration.
The state relied on a Confucian bureaucratic tradition and civil service examinations to staff administration and reinforce centralized imperial rule.
Compare: What is one useful dimension for comparing state power in Song China and the Inca state?
Possible dimensions include bureaucracy/administration, labor or tribute systems, infrastructure, ideology, or methods of integrating diverse populations.
Common AP weakness: naming a state feature without explaining how it helped build, legitimize, or maintain power.
02
Networks of Exchangec. 1200–1450 · Silk Roads, Mongols, Indian Ocean, trans-Saharan trade, diffusion and disease.
8–10%CausationConnections

Big question

Why did long-distance trade expand, and how did expanding networks move more than goods?

Anchor connections

Commercial technologies and state support increased exchange; the Mongol Empire helped connect large parts of Eurasia; merchants and travelers carried religions, crops, technologies, and disease across networks.

NetworkUseful anchorThink about
Silk RoadsCaravan trade across EurasiaLuxury goods, states/nomads, caravan cities
Indian OceanMonsoon-based maritime tradePorts, merchant diasporas, navigation
Trans-SaharanCaravan trade across North/West AfricaGold, salt, Islam, political growth
Causation: Name one factor that increased long-distance trade and one effect of that expansion.
For example, improved commercial practices or transportation technologies helped expand trade; an effect could be greater cultural diffusion, growth of trading cities, or wider spread of epidemic disease.
Connection: Why is the Mongol Empire relevant to both political history and trade history?
Mongol conquest created a vast political zone across Eurasia, while Mongol rule also facilitated movement and exchange across connected routes.
Common AP weakness: treating trade networks as isolated routes. AP questions often focus on how political power, technology, migration, culture, and environment interacted with exchange.
03
Land-Based Empiresc. 1450–1750 · Ottoman, Safavid, Mughal, Qing and other expanding states.
12–15%ComparisonGovernance

Big question

How did expanding land empires acquire territory, administer diverse populations, and legitimize rule?

Anchor connections

Gunpowder weapons aided expansion; rulers used bureaucracies, taxation, military systems, religion, monumental art/architecture, and elite recruitment to consolidate power.

Compare: Why is comparing Ottoman, Safavid, and Mughal religious policy more useful than simply labeling all three “Islamic empires”?
Their rulers governed different populations and used religion differently for legitimacy, administration, and identity. Comparison reveals important differences inside a shared broad category.
Governance: Give one nonmilitary way an empire could maintain authority.
Examples include bureaucracy, taxation, elite recruitment, religious legitimation, monumental architecture, law, or negotiated relationships with local elites.
Common AP weakness: writing “gunpowder caused empire.” Weapons were one factor; administration and legitimacy explain how rule persisted after conquest.
04
Transoceanic Interconnectionsc. 1450–1750 · exploration, maritime empires, Columbian Exchange, Atlantic slavery, new hierarchies.
12–15%CausationCCOT

Big question

How did oceanic expansion reshape economies, populations, environments, state power, and social hierarchies?

Anchor connections

Navigation and state investment enabled maritime expansion; the Columbian Exchange transformed ecologies and populations; Atlantic slavery expanded; maritime empires built new economic systems and racial/social hierarchies.

Causation: Why is the Columbian Exchange both an environmental and demographic development?
Plants, animals, pathogens, and people moved across oceans, changing ecosystems, diets, labor systems, and population patterns—especially through epidemic disease in the Americas.
CCOT: What continuity could remain even while Atlantic trade systems transformed economies?
Examples might include continued use of coerced labor in varied forms, continued importance of older regional trade networks, or persistence of local social structures alongside new Atlantic systems.
Common AP weakness: making this unit only about explorers. The stronger story is the system created after contact: exchange, labor, empire, environment, and hierarchy.
05
Revolutionsc. 1750–1900 · Enlightenment, political revolutions, industrialization, economic and social change.
12–15%CausationComparison

Big question

How did new ideas and industrial production challenge established political, social, and economic systems?

Anchor connections

Enlightenment ideas shaped political challenges; revolutions produced new states and debates over rights; industrialization changed production, urbanization, labor, class relations, and global economic power.

Compare: What makes comparing the Atlantic revolutions historically useful?
They shared some ideological language and broader contexts but differed in participants, social structures, goals, violence, slavery, and political outcomes.
Causation: Give one reason industrialization began earlier in some places than others.
Possible factors include access to capital/resources, agricultural changes, labor, markets, transportation, institutions, and technological development. Strong answers connect the factor to industrial growth.
Common AP weakness: treating “revolution” as one type of event. Political revolutions and industrial revolution involved different mechanisms, even when they interacted.
06
Consequences of Industrializationc. 1750–1900 · imperialism, economic domination, resistance, migration, global labor systems.
12–15%CausationEvidence

Big question

How did industrial power reshape imperial expansion, global economic relationships, migration, and resistance?

Anchor connections

Industrial states pursued resources/markets and strategic power; formal and informal imperialism expanded; colonized peoples resisted; migration created new diasporas and labor systems.

Causation: How could industrialization increase imperial expansion?
Industrial economies increased demand for raw materials and markets while industrial technologies strengthened transportation, communication, and military capacity.
Migration: Why should migration after 1750 be studied as both an economic and social process?
Economic demand and labor systems shaped movement, while migrants also formed communities, changed family/gender patterns, and sometimes faced exclusion or ethnic conflict.
Common AP weakness: reducing imperialism to a single motive. Economic, political, strategic, ideological, and technological factors could reinforce one another.
07
Global Conflictc. 1900–present · world wars, interwar instability, total war, mass atrocities, changing political order.
8–10%CausationContext

Big question

Why did conflicts become increasingly global and destructive, and how did states and societies mobilize for total war?

Anchor connections

Imperial rivalry, nationalism, alliances, and militarization contributed to WWI; interwar crises destabilized politics; WWII mobilized economies and civilians on a vast scale and included genocide and other mass atrocities.

Causation: Why is “the assassination caused World War I” an incomplete explanation?
The assassination was an immediate trigger, but broader conditions such as alliances, militarization, nationalism, imperial competition, and crisis diplomacy help explain why the conflict expanded.
Total war: What makes total war more than a battlefield concept?
Governments mobilize economies, labor, propaganda, technology, and civilian populations, blurring boundaries between military and home fronts.
Common AP weakness: writing chronology instead of causation. A list of events is not an explanation of why war developed or why outcomes mattered.
08
Cold War & Decolonizationc. 1900–present · superpower rivalry, communism, independence movements, new states, end of Cold War.
8–10%ComparisonCCOT

Big question

How did Cold War rivalry and decolonization reshape political power while newly independent states pursued their own goals?

Anchor connections

US–Soviet rivalry operated through alliances, proxy conflicts, arms competition, and ideology; Asian/African independence movements used varied strategies; new states faced political and economic challenges shaped by colonial legacies and global pressures.

Compare: Why is it misleading to treat every decolonization movement as the same process?
Movements differed in colonial ruler, leadership, ideology, use of negotiation or violence, international context, social divisions, and post-independence outcomes.
Connection: How could the Cold War shape decolonization without fully controlling it?
Superpowers supplied aid, pressure, alliances, or intervention, but nationalist leaders and local movements had their own goals, conflicts, and strategies.
Common AP weakness: reducing post-1945 history to “capitalism vs communism.” Decolonization, nationalism, nonalignment, ethnicity, economic development, and local politics also mattered.
09
Globalizationc. 1900–present · technology, environment, disease, economics, reform, culture, resistance, institutions.
8–10%CCOTConnections

Big question

How did intensified global connectivity transform economies, culture, politics, health, and environmental relationships—and what remained uneven or contested?

Anchor connections

Communication and transportation accelerated exchange; global institutions expanded; economies became more interconnected; global culture circulated more rapidly; reform movements and resistance responded to inequalities and perceived costs of globalization.

CCOT: What changed about global exchange after 1900 compared with earlier trade networks?
The speed, scale, volume, and technological infrastructure of exchange increased dramatically, while older patterns such as unequal access and cultural adaptation continued in new forms.
Evaluate: Why can globalization produce both cultural convergence and cultural resistance?
Global media, migration, markets, and institutions spread shared forms and practices, while communities may also defend, revive, or reinterpret local identities in response.
Common AP weakness: writing “technology made the world connected” without naming the technology, mechanism, scale of change, or uneven consequences.
AP World writing studio

Build the historical move before building the paragraph.

ComparisonChoose a shared dimension first: governance, labor, belief, economy, technology, gender, social hierarchy, etc.
CausationSeparate long-term conditions, immediate triggers, mechanisms, and consequences.
Continuity & changeName both what changed and what persisted, then explain why the pattern matters.
ContextualizationGive a broader development that genuinely explains the prompt—not a random fact from the same century.
SourcingUse author, audience, purpose, or situation only when you can explain how it affects the source’s meaning or usefulness.
ArgumentationMake a claim that answers the prompt’s historical reasoning task and organize evidence around it.

01
Functions, transformations & inversesRecognize families, describe key attributes, transform graphs, connect inverse pairs.
A2.2AA2.2BA2.2CA2.2DA2.7I

Big idea

A function is more than an equation: it is a relationship with a domain, range, graph, and behavior. An inverse reverses the input-output relationship, which is why the graphs of inverse functions reflect across y=x.

Prerequisite check

Can you evaluate function notation, read points from a graph, and solve a simple equation for a variable? If not, repair that first.

Transformation decoder

What you seeWhat it doesFast self-check
a·f(x)Vertical stretch/compression; negative a reflects across x-axis.Output values change.
f(x)+dShift up/down.Every y-value changes by d.
f(x−c)Shift right/left.Horizontal shifts look “backward” inside.
f(bx)Horizontal compression/stretch; negative b reflects across y-axis.Inputs are being altered.
1
Worked example: Compare g(x)=−2√(x−3)+4 with f(x)=√x.
2
x−3 shifts the square-root graph right 3, so its endpoint moves from (0,0) to (3,0).
3
The factor −2 reflects across the x-axis and stretches vertically by 2.
4
+4 shifts everything up 4. Endpoint: (3,4). Real domain: x≥3. Range: y≤4.
Mistake clinic: “x−3 means left 3.” This comes from treating the inside like an outside shift. Ask instead: “What x-value makes the inside equal the parent input 0?” Here, x−3=0 gives x=3.

Retrieval + practice ladder

Retrieve: Why must a quadratic usually have a restricted domain before its inverse is a function?
Without a restriction, most horizontal lines hit the parabola twice. Restricting to one side makes the quadratic one-to-one so the inverse passes the vertical-line test.
Level 1: For h(x)=3|x+2|−5, identify the vertex.
Vertex (−2, −5).
Level 2: If f(4)=9, what point must appear on the graph of f⁻¹?
(9,4), because inverse functions swap inputs and outputs.
Level 3: Explain why exponential and logarithmic graphs have opposite types of asymptotes.
They are inverse functions, so reflection across y=x swaps horizontal and vertical features. A horizontal asymptote of an exponential becomes a vertical asymptote of its logarithmic inverse.
02
Systems of equations & inequalitiesModel situations, choose a method, solve three-variable and linear/quadratic systems, interpret solution sets.
A2.3AA2.3BA2.3CA2.3DA2.3E–G

Big idea

A solution to a system must make every equation or inequality true. Graphically, you are looking for shared points or shared regions.

Prerequisite check

Can you solve a linear equation, substitute an expression, distribute correctly, and graph a linear inequality with the correct boundary?

Choose a method on purpose

If the system looks like…Try…Why
One variable already isolatedSubstitutionThe replacement is ready.
Coefficients line up or can line up quicklyEliminationYou can remove a variable efficiently.
Three linear equationsGaussian elimination / matrix technology / substitutionOrganization matters more as variables increase.
One linear + one quadraticSubstitutionReplace y or x and solve the resulting quadratic.
Several inequalitiesGraph + overlapThe solution is a region, not just one point.
1
Worked example: Solve y=x+2 and y=x²−4.
2
Set the two expressions for y equal: x+2=x²−4.
3
Rearrange: x²−x−6=0 → (x−3)(x+2)=0, so x=3 or x=−2.
4
Use y=x+2: points are (3,5) and (−2,0). Two solutions mean the line intersects the parabola twice.
Mistake clinic: finding x-values and stopping. A system solution is an ordered pair (or ordered triple). Substitute back and report the full solution.
Retrieve: What does “no solution” mean graphically for two equations?
The graphs have no shared point. For two distinct parallel lines, for example, there is no intersection.
Level 1: Best first method for y=2x+1 and 3x+y=16?
Substitution, because y is already isolated.
Level 2: A point lies in the overlap of two shaded inequality regions but lies on a dashed boundary. Can it be a solution if that dashed line belongs to a strict inequality?
No. A dashed line means the boundary itself is excluded.
Level 3: Why can a line and a parabola have 0, 1, or 2 real solutions?
The line can miss the parabola, touch it once (tangent), or cross it twice.

Quadratics & square-root equationsMove among forms, choose a solving method, interpret parabolas, handle radicals, reject extraneous solutions.
A2.4AA2.4BA2.4CA2.4DA2.4E–H

Big idea

Different forms reveal different information: standard form highlights y-intercept and coefficients, factored form reveals zeros, and vertex form reveals the vertex and axis of symmetry.

Prerequisite check

Can you factor basic trinomials, simplify radicals, and solve equations? These are the usual hidden causes of quadratic difficulty.

1
Worked example: Solve 2x²+3x−7=0. Factoring is not obvious, so use the quadratic formula.
2
a=2, b=3, c=−7. The discriminant is 3²−4(2)(−7)=65.
3
x=(−3±√65)/4. Since 65 is not a perfect square, leave exact answers in radical form unless decimal approximations are requested.
Mistake clinic: forgetting ± in the square-root method. If x²=25, both 5 and −5 square to 25.
Radical warning: squaring both sides can create a solution that did not satisfy the original equation. Always substitute candidates back into the original square-root equation.
Retrieve: Which form of a quadratic most directly shows the vertex?
Vertex form: a(x−h)²+k, with vertex (h,k).
Level 1: Solve (x+1)²=16.
x+1=±4, so x=3 or x=−5.
Level 2: For x²−4x−5=0, choose a method and solve.
Factor: (x−5)(x+1)=0, so x=5 or x=−1.
Level 3: Without solving completely, how many real solutions does x²+2x+7=0 have?
Zero real solutions because b²−4ac=4−28=−24, which is negative. The solutions are complex.
04
Exponential & logarithmic functionsTranslate between inverse forms, model growth/decay, transform graphs, solve equations, check logarithmic domains.
A2.5AA2.5BA2.5CA2.5DA2.5E

Big idea

A logarithm answers an exponent question. log₂8=3 and 2³=8 are the same relationship written from opposite directions.

Prerequisite check

Can you use exponent rules, distinguish additive change from multiplicative change, and evaluate powers accurately?

Translation bridge

Logarithmic formExponential formMeaning

log₁₀0.01=−210⁻²=0.01Negative exponent means reciprocal power.
ln(e⁵)=5e⁵=e⁵ln and eˣ undo each other.

Worked model: A population starts at 500 and increases 8% per year.
2

Domain warning: the argument of a real logarithm must be positive. Any algebraic candidate that makes the log input zero or negative must be rejected.
Retrieve: What kind of change suggests an exponential model?
A roughly constant multiplicative or percentage change over equal intervals.
Level 1: Rewrite log₅125=3 exponentially.
5³=125.
Level 2: A value decreases 12% each year. What is the decay factor?
0.88, because 1−0.12=0.88.
Level 3: Why does log(x−4)=2 require x>4 even before solving?
The logarithm’s argument x−4 must be positive, so x−4>0 → x>4.

These families share transformation rules, but their shapes and solving logic differ. Absolute value encodes distance, which is why equations often split into two cases.

Can you isolate an expression and solve linear equations/inequalities? Can you reverse an inequality when multiplying or dividing by a negative?

Absolute value represents a distance and cannot be negative.

06
Rational functions, expressions & inverse variationTrack restrictions, simplify legally, solve rational equations, interpret asymptotes, model inverse variation.
A2.6GA2.6HA2.6IA2.6JA2.6KA2.6LA2.7F

Big idea

Restrictions are not side notes. They are part of the function. Any input that makes a denominator zero is excluded even if later algebra appears to cancel that factor.

Prerequisite check

Can you factor polynomials and find a least common denominator? Rational-expression errors often begin there.

Legal algebra check

MoveLegal?Reason
(x²−9)/(x−3) → x+3, x≠3YesFactor first; cancel a common factor and preserve restriction.
(x+3)/(x+5) → 3/5NoYou cannot cancel terms across addition.
Multiply a rational equation by the LCDYesBut check final answers against excluded values.
1
Worked example: Solve 1/x + 1/3 = 1.
2
Restriction: x≠0. LCD is 3x. Multiply every term by 3x.
3
3+x=3x → 3=2x → x=3/2, which is allowed.
Mistake clinic: canceling before factoring, or forgetting that an original excluded value stays excluded even if a factor cancels.
Retrieve: What input is excluded from f(x)=4/(x−7)?
x=7.
Level 1: Simplify (x²−16)/(x−4) and state the restriction.
x+4, with x≠4.
Level 2: If y varies inversely with x and y=6 when x=4, find k in y=k/x.
24, because k=xy=24.
Level 3: Explain the difference between a hole and a vertical asymptote after factoring a rational function.
A canceled common factor typically creates a removable discontinuity (hole); a denominator factor that remains after simplification creates a vertical asymptote.

08
Data, regression & mathematical modelingSelect linear/quadratic/exponential models, use regression technology, interpret parameters, make predictions and judgments.
A2.8AA2.8BA2.8CA2.1A–G

Big idea

Regression is not “press buttons and copy the equation.” You must choose a plausible family, interpret the model in context, and judge whether a prediction is reasonable.

Prerequisite check

Can you distinguish constant difference from constant ratio/percentage change and recognize the curved symmetry of a quadratic pattern?

Model selector

Data patternLikely modelClue
Roughly constant first differencesLinearAdd about the same amount each step.
Roughly constant second differencesQuadraticRate of change changes linearly.
Roughly constant ratios / percent changeExponentialMultiply by about the same factor each step.
1
Worked reasoning: Values 100, 120, 144, 172.8 grow by 20% each step.
2
Differences are not constant, but the ratio is consistently 1.2.
3
An exponential model is appropriate: y=100(1.2)ˣ if x=0 corresponds to the first value.
Mistake clinic: choosing a model solely because its R² is slightly larger while ignoring the context or shape. A good model must make mathematical and situational sense.
Retrieve: Constant percentage change usually indicates what family?
Exponential.
Level 1: The sequence 4, 9, 14, 19 has what likely model?
Linear, because first differences are constantly +5.
Level 2: Why is predicting far beyond the observed data range risky?
Extrapolation assumes the relationship continues outside the region where evidence was collected; that assumption may fail.
Level 3: A quadratic regression fits a ball’s height over time. What physical feature might the vertex represent?
The maximum height, if the parabola opens downward and the modeled interval includes the peak.

Matter, measurement & evidenceClassify matter, distinguish physical/chemical change, use density and measurement, reason from evidence.
§112.43MatterScientific practices

Big idea

Matter is identified and compared using measurable properties. A chemical change changes particle identity; a physical change changes form, state, or arrangement without creating a new substance.

Diagnostic question

Are you using the words “physical” and “chemical” because of what something looks like, or because you have evidence that particle identity changed?

Evidence sorter

ObservationWhat it can suggestWhat it does not prove by itself
Melting / freezingState changeNew substance formation

1
Worked reasoning: A 24.0 g metal sample occupies 3.00 mL. Density = mass/volume.
2
24.0 g ÷ 3.00 mL = 8.00 g/mL.
3
The units are part of the answer: density describes mass per unit volume.
Mistake clinic: treating every color change as automatic proof of a chemical reaction. Color can be evidence, but the claim should be tied to the full context and additional observations when possible.
Retrieve: Why is boiling water a physical change?
The particles are still H₂O; their spacing and motion change as liquid becomes gas, but the substance identity does not.
Level 1: A sample has mass 45 g and volume 15 mL. Find density.
3 g/mL.
Level 2: Why should repeated measurements be considered when deciding whether an unexpected value is an error?
Repeated measurements help distinguish random variation from a consistent pattern or systematic problem.
02
Atomic theory & electron structureConnect models of the atom to evidence, distinguish particles/isotopes/ions, and reason about electron arrangements.
§112.43Atomic theoryModels

Big idea

Atomic models are not tiny photographs. They are explanatory models revised when evidence requires a better description of matter.

Keep the counts separate

Atomic number = protons. Mass number = protons + neutrons. Ion charge depends on the difference between protons and electrons.

If this changes…You get…
Number of protonsA different element
Number of neutronsA different isotope of the same element
Number of electronsAn ion of the same element
1
Example: A particle has 17 protons, 18 electrons, and 18 neutrons.
2
17 protons identifies chlorine. 18 electrons means one more electron than protons, so charge = −1.
3
Mass number = 17+18=35, so this is chlorine-35 as a 1− ion.
Mistake clinic: saying an atom “gains a proton to become negative.” Ordinary ion formation changes electrons, not the nucleus.
Retrieve: What makes two atoms isotopes of the same element?
They have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.
Level 1: An ion has 12 protons and 10 electrons. What is its charge?
2+.
Level 2: Why do atomic models change over time?
New evidence can reveal limitations in an older model, so scientists revise models to explain observations more accurately.
03
Periodic table & periodic trendsUse table position and electron behavior to explain—not just memorize—trends and chemical similarities.
§112.43Periodic tablePatterns

Big idea

The periodic table is a pattern map. Elements in the same group often share valence-electron patterns, helping explain similar chemical behavior.

Reasoning habit

When comparing trends, ask about nuclear attraction, shielding, occupied energy levels, and the distance between valence electrons and the nucleus.

Trend story: Across a period, increasing nuclear charge generally pulls electrons more strongly while the principal energy level remains the same. Down a group, additional occupied energy levels increase distance and shielding.
Mistake clinic: memorizing “up and right” arrows without understanding what property the arrow represents. Different periodic properties trend differently; always name the property first.
Retrieve: Why do elements in the same group often react similarly?
They often have similar valence-electron arrangements, which strongly influence bonding and reactions.
Level 1: Which usually has more occupied energy levels: an element near the top of a group or near the bottom?
An element lower in the group.
Level 2: Why is explaining a trend stronger than only drawing an arrow?
The explanation connects the observed pattern to atomic structure and forces, making the trend usable in unfamiliar comparisons.
04
Bonding, formulas & structureDistinguish ionic/covalent bonding, write formulas, reason from valence electrons, and connect structure to properties.
§112.43Chemical bondingStructure/property

Big idea

Bonding is about electrostatic interactions and electron arrangement. Structure helps explain properties such as conductivity, melting behavior, and solubility.

Formula check

For a neutral ionic compound, total positive charge and total negative charge must balance.

1
Formula example: aluminum forms Al³⁺ and oxygen forms O²⁻.
2
Smallest common charge magnitude is 6: two Al³⁺ give +6 and three O²⁻ give −6.
3
Formula: Al₂O₃.
Mistake clinic: “Ionic means electrons are literally sitting halfway between ions.” Ionic and covalent models describe different patterns of electron distribution and attraction; do not mix their particle pictures.
Retrieve: What must be true about the total charge of a neutral ionic compound?
The positive and negative charges must sum to zero.
Level 1: Write the formula formed by Mg²⁺ and Cl⁻.
MgCl₂.
Level 2: Why is structure useful for predicting properties?
How particles and charges are arranged determines what attractions must be overcome and whether charged particles can move, influencing properties such as melting and conductivity.
05
Reactions & stoichiometryBalance equations, interpret coefficients, convert among quantities, identify limiting reactants, and reason about yield.
§112.43StoichiometryConservation

Big idea

A balanced chemical equation is a particle-ratio statement. Stoichiometry works because those coefficients connect amounts of reactants and products.

Mole-map habit

If converting between different substances, pass through moles and use the balanced-equation mole ratio.

Stoichiometry route

1
Example: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. How many moles H₂O can form from 3.0 mol O₂ with excess H₂?
2
Use coefficient ratio: 3.0 mol O₂ × (2 mol H₂O / 1 mol O₂).

4

Mistake clinic: using coefficients as gram ratios. Coefficients directly represent particle/mole ratios; mass relationships require molar masses.
Conservation warning: balance equations by changing coefficients, never subscripts. Changing a subscript changes the substance.
Retrieve: In N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃, what is the mole ratio H₂:NH₃?
3:2.
Level 1: Using the same equation, how many moles NH₃ form from 6 mol H₂ with excess N₂?
4 mol NH₃.
Level 2: What determines the limiting reactant?
The reactant that is consumed first according to the stoichiometric ratios, limiting how much product can form.
Level 3: Why can percent yield be below 100% even if the balanced equation is correct?
Real processes can lose product, have incomplete reaction, include side reactions, or include measurement/transfer limitations.
06
Gases & particle-motion reasoningConnect pressure, volume, temperature, and amount to particle motion and use gas relationships with consistent units.
§112.43Gas lawsParticle model

Big idea

Gas laws are not arbitrary formulas. They summarize how particle collisions change when temperature, volume, pressure, or amount changes.

Temperature rule

Gas-law temperature calculations require an absolute temperature scale. Convert Celsius to kelvin when the equation requires temperature.

1
Reason first: A sealed flexible container is warmed while external pressure stays roughly constant.
2
Particles move faster on average as temperature rises.
3
To keep pressure from rising substantially, the flexible container can expand, increasing volume and reducing collision frequency per area.
Mistake clinic: plugging Celsius directly into a proportional gas-law calculation. Zero Celsius is not zero particle thermal energy, so Celsius does not work as an absolute proportional scale.
Retrieve: At constant temperature, what generally happens to pressure if volume decreases?
Pressure increases because particles collide with container walls more frequently per unit area.
Level 1: Convert 27°C to kelvin.
About 300 K.
Level 2: Why does adding gas particles to a rigid container tend to increase pressure if temperature stays constant?
More particles create more wall collisions in the same volume.
07
Solutions, concentration & acid-base chemistryReason about dissolving, concentration, dilution, pH, acids/bases, and solution evidence.
§112.43SolutionsAcid-base

Big idea

Concentration describes amount of solute relative to solution quantity. Dilution changes concentration by adding solvent without changing the amount of solute transferred.

Acid-base habit

Do not reduce acid-base chemistry to “pH below 7 / above 7.” Connect pH to hydrogen-ion behavior and evidence from indicators or measurements.

1
Dilution example: 50.0 mL of 2.0 M solution is diluted to 200.0 mL.
2
Using M₁V₁=M₂V₂: (2.0)(50.0)=M₂(200.0).
3
M₂=0.50 M. Four times the volume gives one-fourth the concentration when solute amount is conserved.
Mistake clinic: saying dilution “removes solute.” In a normal dilution, solvent is added; the amount of solute in the transferred sample remains the same while total volume increases.
Retrieve: If the same amount of solute is placed in twice the solution volume, what happens to concentration?
It becomes half as concentrated.
Level 1: Which is more acidic: pH 3 or pH 6?
pH 3.
Level 2: Why should a concentration answer include units such as mol/L?
The units communicate the ratio being measured—amount of solute per volume of solution.
08
Thermochemistry & nuclear chemistryTrack energy transfer, distinguish system/surroundings, reason about endo/exothermic processes, and compare nuclear with chemical change.
§112.43ThermochemistryNuclear chemistry

Thermochemistry big idea

Temperature change is evidence of energy transfer. Always identify the system and surroundings before saying energy is “gained” or “lost.”

Nuclear big idea

Chemical reactions rearrange electrons and atoms; nuclear processes change the nucleus and can change one element into another.

ProcessSystem energy directionTypical surroundings observation
ExothermicEnergy transferred out of systemSurroundings may warm
EndothermicEnergy transferred into systemSurroundings may cool
1
Heat calculation: q=mcΔT links heat transfer to sample mass, specific heat, and temperature change.
2
If a substance warms, ΔT=Tfinal−Tinitial is positive for that substance.
3
Interpretation still depends on what you defined as the system.
Mistake clinic: “Exothermic means the reaction gets cold.” Exothermic describes energy leaving the system; the surroundings commonly warm because they receive that energy.
Retrieve: What does a negative ΔT mean for the measured sample?
Its final temperature is lower than its initial temperature.
Level 1: What major part of the atom changes in a nuclear process but not an ordinary chemical reaction?
The nucleus.
Level 2: Why must “system” be defined before interpreting heat flow?
Energy leaving one defined system enters its surroundings; the sign/direction statement depends on which part you are tracking.

Deep study modules

Eight units, each paired with AP-style thinking.

01
Chemistry of LifeWater, macromolecules, molecular interactions, and why structure supports function.
8–11%ConceptsVisuals

Big idea

Biological properties emerge from molecular structure and interactions. Polarity, hydrogen bonding, functional groups, and macromolecular structure help explain what molecules do in cells.

AP move

Do not stop at “water is polar.” Explain how polarity causes an interaction and how that interaction produces a biological consequence.

1
Mechanism chain: Water molecules are polar.
2
Partial charges allow hydrogen bonds between neighboring water molecules.
3
Large amounts of energy are required to disrupt many of those interactions.
4
Biological consequence: water can resist rapid temperature change, helping stabilize environments and organisms.
Common AP weakness: naming a property but skipping the causal middle. “Hydrogen bonds cause temperature stability” is stronger when you explain that energy must disrupt intermolecular attractions before molecular motion increases substantially.
Concept: Why can a change in protein shape alter protein function?
Protein function depends on three-dimensional structure. A shape change can alter binding sites or interactions needed for the protein’s role.
Argument: A mutation replaces a nonpolar amino acid in a protein core with a charged amino acid. Predict one possible consequence and justify it.
The substitution may disrupt hydrophobic interactions or create unfavorable charge interactions, changing folding and therefore protein function. The exact effect depends on location and surrounding residues.
02
CellsCell structure, membranes, transport, compartmentalization, and surface-area-to-volume reasoning.
10–13%ModelsData

Big idea

Cells maintain internal conditions by controlling movement across membranes and by compartmentalizing processes into specialized spaces.

AP move

When asked about membrane transport, identify the substance, its gradient, membrane permeability, and whether energy or a protein is involved.

Surface-area-to-volume: as a cell becomes larger, volume increases faster than surface area. That can make exchange across the membrane less efficient relative to cellular demand.
Common AP weakness: “Water moves to high concentration.” High concentration of what? Osmosis reasoning should specify water potential/solute context rather than using an ambiguous concentration phrase.
Visual reasoning: A cell is placed in a solution and loses mass. What does that suggest about net water movement?
Net water movement was out of the cell under the tested conditions.
Experimental design: What would be a useful dependent variable in an osmosis investigation using plant tissue?
A measurable change such as percent change in mass of the tissue after exposure to different solutions.
Mechanism: Why does increasing membrane folds help some cells?
Folding increases membrane surface area available for exchange or membrane-associated reactions without requiring a proportional increase in cell volume.
03
Cellular EnergeticsEnzymes, photosynthesis, respiration, energy transfer, and experimental evidence about rates.
12–16%ExperimentsData analysis

Big idea

Cells transform energy through coupled reactions and electron transfers. Enzymes influence reaction rates by lowering activation energy without changing the overall free-energy difference of the reaction.

AP move

When interpreting rate data, separate rate from amount. A graph can level off after rapid early change without meaning the reaction “reversed.”

1
Enzyme experiment: Reaction rate rises with substrate concentration and then plateaus.
2
At low substrate levels, more substrate increases enzyme-substrate encounters.
3
At high substrate levels, available active sites become a limiting factor, so adding more substrate has less effect on rate.
Common AP weakness: saying enzymes “give reactions energy.” Enzymes change the activation-energy barrier; they do not supply the reaction’s net energy.
Concept: Why can very high temperature reduce enzyme activity?
High temperature can disrupt interactions that maintain protein structure, altering the active site and reducing effective binding/catalysis.
Data: Oxygen consumption increases in germinating seeds compared with non-germinating seeds. What process could explain the difference?
Greater cellular respiration in actively growing/germinating tissue can increase oxygen consumption.
Method: Why include a control condition in an enzyme-rate experiment?
It provides a baseline for comparison so observed differences can be more confidently associated with the manipulated variable.
04
Cell Communication & Cell CycleSignal reception/transduction/response, feedback, checkpoints, and consequences of disrupted regulation.
10–15%ModelsMechanisms

Big idea

Cells detect information, transmit it through molecular interactions, and change behavior. Regulation depends on both signaling pathways and controlled progression through the cell cycle.

AP move

Trace the pathway in order. If a component is blocked, predict what happens to downstream steps—not just the blocked molecule itself.

Signal pathway frame: reception → transduction → cellular response. Amplification may occur during transduction, and feedback can alter pathway activity.
Mechanism: If a receptor cannot bind its ligand, what is one likely consequence?
The downstream signal-transduction pathway may not activate, reducing or preventing the expected cellular response.
Prediction: A mutation makes a growth-signaling protein permanently active. Predict a possible cell-cycle consequence.
Cells may receive a persistent proliferative signal, increasing inappropriate cell-cycle progression if other controls do not stop it.
05
HeredityMeiosis, inheritance, probability, chromosome behavior, and sources of genetic variation.
8–11%ProbabilityData

Big idea

Inheritance patterns emerge from chromosome behavior during meiosis and the probabilistic combination of alleles during fertilization.

AP move

Use probability as evidence about expected patterns, but remember that a probability does not guarantee the exact outcome in a small sample.

Common AP weakness: saying meiosis “creates mutations” as its main source of variation. Independent assortment and crossing over reshuffle existing alleles; mutation is a separate source of new genetic variation.
Concept: How does crossing over increase variation?
Homologous chromosomes exchange corresponding DNA segments, generating new combinations of alleles on chromatids.
Probability: If an event has probability 1/4, does that mean exactly one of every four offspring must show it?
No. Probability describes expectation over many trials; small samples can deviate by chance.
Mechanism: Why can nondisjunction change chromosome number in gametes?
Chromosomes or sister chromatids fail to separate normally, causing some gametes to receive extra copies and others to receive too few.
06
Gene Expression & RegulationDNA information flow, gene regulation, mutation effects, biotechnology, and differential expression.
12–16%ModelsArgumentation

Big idea

Cells can contain the same DNA yet behave differently because genes are regulated differently. Information flows through transcription and translation, while regulatory systems influence when and how much gene product is made.

AP move

For a mutation question, locate the change first: regulatory region, coding sequence, splice site, etc. Then predict the molecular consequence before jumping to the organismal phenotype.

1
Mutation reasoning: A base substitution changes one codon.
2
The effect depends on the new codon: it could code for the same amino acid, a different amino acid, or a stop signal.
3
The protein-level effect then depends on location and structural/functional importance.
Common AP weakness: “A mutation changes the organism.” Some mutations are silent, occur in noncritical regions, or have effects only in certain environments or tissues.
Concept: How can two cell types in one organism contain the same genome but make different proteins?
Different genes can be turned on or off, producing different patterns of gene expression.
Method: If you want to test whether a gene is transcribed more under one condition, what type of evidence would be useful?
A measurement related to RNA abundance for that gene, compared across controlled conditions.
Argument: Why is a frameshift often more disruptive than a single conservative amino-acid substitution?
A frameshift can alter many downstream codons and may introduce a premature stop, whereas a conservative substitution may alter only one amino acid with similar chemical properties.
07
Natural SelectionSelection, population change, evolutionary evidence, phylogeny, speciation, and other mechanisms of evolution.
13–20%EvidenceArgumentation

Big idea

Evolution is change in populations across generations. Natural selection changes the frequency of heritable traits when individuals differ in survival or reproductive success under particular environmental conditions.

AP move

Do not write that an individual “evolved because it needed to.” Identify preexisting heritable variation, a selective pressure, differential reproductive success, and a population-level frequency change.

1
Selection chain: A population contains heritable variation in a trait.
2
Environmental conditions cause individuals with some variants to leave more offspring on average.
3
Alleles associated with those variants can become more common across generations.
Common AP weakness: using “stronger” or “better” without defining fitness in context. Evolutionary fitness is tied to reproductive contribution under specific conditions.
Concept: Why must a trait difference be heritable for natural selection to produce evolutionary change in that trait?
If the trait difference is not transmitted to offspring, differential success will not reliably change the trait’s genetic frequency across generations.
Evidence: What kind of result would support the claim that an allele is increasing under selection?
Repeated population data showing the allele frequency increasing across generations under the relevant conditions, ideally with evidence connecting the allele/trait to differential reproductive success.
Phylogeny: What does a shared recent node on a phylogenetic tree indicate?
The lineages share a more recent common ancestor relative to lineages whose common node is farther back.
08
EcologyPopulation dynamics, species interactions, energy flow, matter cycling, ecosystems, and biodiversity.
10–15%DataSystems

Big idea

Ecological systems are shaped by flows of energy, cycling of matter, population constraints, interactions among organisms, and disturbances across scales.

AP move

When interpreting an ecological graph, identify the level of organization first: individual, population, community, or ecosystem. That prevents mixing causes and effects across scales.

Energy vs. matter: matter cycles through ecosystems; energy flows through them and is transformed, with usable energy decreasing across trophic transfers.
Common AP weakness: saying energy “cycles” the same way carbon or nitrogen does. Matter can be reused through biogeochemical cycles; energy flow is directional.
Concept: Why can a population not grow exponentially forever in a finite environment?
Resources and other density-dependent or environmental constraints eventually limit growth.
Data: A predator population rises after its prey population rises. Why is timing important before claiming direct causation?
Population responses can lag, and other variables may affect both populations. Temporal association alone is not complete proof of a direct causal mechanism.
Systems: Why can removing one species affect several other populations?
Species are linked through food webs, competition, mutualisms, and other interactions, so changing one interaction can propagate through the community.
FRQ reasoning studio

Write the sentence that earns the point.

Describe

Say what the evidence shows.

Use direction, comparison, magnitude when available, and the variables actually shown. Do not explain cause unless the prompt asks you to.

Explain

Give the biological mechanism.

Connect cause → process → consequence. Avoid replacing the mechanism with a vocabulary word.

Predict

State a directional outcome and why.

Make the prediction explicit before the justification so the reader never has to infer your answer.

Justify

Use evidence that actually supports the claim.

Name the relevant evidence, then explain the biological connection. Evidence without reasoning is unfinished.

Mini-FRQ: A researcher finds that cells exposed to treatment X have lower ATP production and lower oxygen consumption than untreated cells. Predict one cellular process that may be directly affected and justify your prediction.
Aerobic cellular respiration may be inhibited. Oxygen is used as the terminal electron acceptor in the electron transport chain, and reduced electron transport would reduce the proton gradient used to drive ATP synthesis, consistent with lower oxygen consumption and ATP production.

Argument: Why can a true statistic still be weak evidence?
It may be irrelevant, outdated, unrepresentative, missing context, or insufficient to support the particular claim being made.
Research: What should you do when two credible sources disagree?
Compare methods, evidence, dates, definitions, scope, and assumptions; investigate the disagreement rather than choosing the source you prefer.
04
Powerful OpeningsAnalyze how openings establish perspective, setting, character, tension, expectations, and direction.
~7 weeksStructureLiterary analysis

Big idea

An opening is a set of promises and controlled absences. Writers decide what readers know first, whose perspective shapes the entry, and which questions remain unanswered.

Question to ask

What expectations does the opening create, and which craft choices create those expectations?

Opening audit: perspective · setting · first conflict/tension · first character impression · first unanswered question · dominant language pattern · what is deliberately withheld.
Common error: summarizing the opening instead of analyzing how it is constructed. Your paragraph should focus on choices and effects, not retell the plot.
Structure: Why might an author delay naming the main character?
Depending on context, delaying the name can create distance, mystery, emphasis on role/condition, or control what readers understand first. The effect must be supported by the text.
Perspective: How can a first-person opening shape reader expectations immediately?
Readers enter through that narrator’s perceptions, vocabulary, judgments, and limitations, which frames what seems important or trustworthy.
Writing repair shop

Fix the paragraph at the level where it broke.

Weak claimMake it arguable and specific enough that evidence could prove or complicate it.
Weak evidenceReplace broad summary with a precise detail, quotation, or source fact that directly matters.
Weak reasoningExplain the relationship between the evidence and the claim. This is often the missing sentence.
Weak cohesionMake the order of ideas visible. Transitions should show relationships, not decorate paragraphs.

01
ArgumentEvaluate lines of reasoning, evidence, assumptions, rhetorical situations, and choices made for particular audiences.
~5 weeksArgumentReasoning

Big idea

An argument is not just a claim plus facts. It is a line of reasoning shaped for an audience under particular circumstances.

Read for pressure

Ask what objection, value, fear, assumption, or competing perspective the writer seems to anticipate.

LayerQuestion
ClaimWhat does the writer want the audience to accept?
EvidenceWhat support is offered, and how credible/relevant is it?
ReasoningWhat logical bridge connects evidence to claim?
AudienceWhich choices seem tailored to this audience’s values or resistance?
CounterpressureWhat alternative view or objection is acknowledged, minimized, or omitted?
Common error: labeling ethos/pathos/logos and stopping. A rhetorical label only becomes analysis when you explain what the choice does for this audience in this situation.
Argument: Why can acknowledging a counterargument strengthen a writer’s position?
It can show awareness of complexity, address audience concerns, clarify the limits of the claim, or create an opportunity to rebut an objection.
Reasoning: What is an assumption in an argument?
An idea the reasoning depends on but may not explicitly prove or state.
02
Persuasion in LiteratureAnalyze how characters persuade, resist, adapt language, and reveal social or cultural conflicts.
~4 weeksFiction/dramaRhetorical situation

Big idea

Persuasion inside fiction and drama is dynamic: speakers respond to other characters, power relationships, changing circumstances, and competing values.

Stage the exchange

Track speaker → audience → goal → resistance → adjustment. Dialogue often reveals persuasion as an evolving interaction, not a prepared speech.

Dialogue lens: What does the speaker want? What does the listener want? What power does each person hold? When does the strategy change? What larger conflict does the exchange represent?
Common error: treating a character’s speech as if the author personally believes every claim inside it. Separate character rhetoric from authorial construction.
Literary rhetoric: Why might a character change from logical explanation to emotional appeal mid-conversation?
The first strategy may not be persuading the listener, or the speaker may recognize that the listener responds more strongly to a different value, fear, relationship, or pressure.
Power: How can unequal status affect persuasion in dialogue?
It can shape what can be said safely, which strategies are available, how direct a speaker can be, and how much resistance the listener can express.
03
Voice in SynthesisCompare perspectives, connect sources, build an original line of reasoning, and keep your own analytical voice visible.
~5 weeksNonfictionSynthesis

Big idea

Synthesis is relationship-making. Sources can agree, complicate, contradict, qualify, extend, or answer one another. Your job is to organize those relationships around your own claim.

Voice test

After removing source names, can a reader still identify your argument? If the paper becomes a pile of source summaries, your voice has disappeared.

Source relationship verbs

RelationshipUseful thinking verb
Sources support a similar idea for different reasonsconverge / reinforce
One source limits another’s broad claimqualify / complicate
Sources reach incompatible conclusionscontradict / challenge
One adds a missing dimensionextend / contextualize
Common error: Source A paragraph → Source B paragraph → Source C paragraph. Organize synthesis by ideas or subclaims so multiple sources can interact inside the same line of reasoning.
Synthesis: Two sources agree on a problem but recommend different solutions. Is that agreement or disagreement?
Both. They converge on the diagnosis but diverge on the response. A strong synthesis states both relationships precisely.
Voice: What keeps your own voice present when using several sources?
A clear controlling claim and line of reasoning that decides why each source appears, how sources relate, and what conclusion follows from the combined evidence.
04
Purpose in Poetry & ProseAnalyze voice, tone, imagery, form, syntax, sound, structure, and how choices work together toward purpose.
~4 weeksPoetry/prosePurpose

Big idea

Purpose is not a magic phrase hidden in the text. It emerges from relationships among speaker, audience, situation, form, tone, and repeated craft choices.

Poetry habit

Read the sentence before analyzing the line break. Then ask what the lineation, sound, or structure adds to the sentence-level meaning.

Purpose chain: craft choice → local effect → pattern/relationship → speaker or writer stance → larger purpose.
Common error: treating tone as one adjective for the entire text. Tone can shift; identify where and how the language changes.
Poetry: Why is paraphrasing a difficult sentence useful before analyzing imagery or sound?
You need a basic grasp of what the sentence is saying before you can explain how formal or language choices reshape, emphasize, complicate, or intensify that meaning.
Tone: What evidence supports a claim that tone shifts?
A change in diction, syntax, imagery, punctuation, speaker focus, rhythm, or another textual pattern at a particular point.
